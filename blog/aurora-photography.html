Aurora photography for beginners

You don't need a 5 000 € rig to bring home a stunning aurora photo. Here is the practical setup that works for almost anyone.

Gear: the minimum that actually works

Any camera that lets you control shutter speed, ISO and aperture manually will work — entry-level mirrorless and DSLR bodies are perfect. The single most important accessory is a sturdy tripod. The aurora moves, but your camera must not.

  • Lens: wide-angle (14–24 mm full-frame, 10–18 mm APS-C) with a fast aperture of f/2.8 or wider when possible.
  • Tripod: stable, with a head that holds position in the cold.
  • Remote or self-timer: avoids vibration when you press the shutter.
  • Spare batteries: cold drains them fast — keep them in an inside pocket.

Starting camera settings

Use these as a baseline, then adjust based on aurora brightness and movement:

  • Mode: Manual (M)
  • Aperture: as wide as your lens allows (f/2.8 or wider)
  • Shutter speed: 2–8 seconds. Faint aurora → longer; fast-dancing aurora → 1–3 s to freeze structure.
  • ISO: 1600–3200 to start. Push higher only if needed.
  • Focus: manual, set to infinity. Test on a bright star with magnified live view.
  • White balance: 3500–4000 K for natural greens.
  • File format: RAW, always.

Shooting with a smartphone

Modern phones (iPhone 12 and newer, Pixel 6+, Samsung S21+) handle aurora surprisingly well in Night Mode. Two rules: keep the phone perfectly still on a small tripod or rest it on a wall, and let the exposure run its full length (often 10–30 seconds). Tap the darkest part of the sky to set focus.

Composition tips

  • Include a foreground — a tree, cabin, frozen lake or person — to give the sky scale.
  • Shoot wide enough to capture the arc but tight enough to avoid empty space.
  • Reflections on water or wet ice double the drama.
  • Avoid bright artificial lights in frame — they blow out instantly at high ISO.

Field workflow

Arrive before dark, find your composition while you can still see, and pre-focus on a distant light. Take a test shot, then leave the camera mostly alone — refocus only if you change focal length. Keep the lens hood on; frost is the enemy. If the front element fogs up, gently warm it with body heat, never breathe on it.

Stay safe and warm

Dress in layers, bring hand warmers, and tell someone where you are going. The best photos rarely happen in the parking lot — but never trade a shot for a dangerous detour onto unplowed roads or thin ice. Patience and warm boots win more aurora nights than expensive gear.
